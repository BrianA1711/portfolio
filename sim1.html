Chapter 01 // Context & Spark

How It Started: Whitechapel in 1888

Victorian London's Divided City Social Context

In the late 1880s, London was the wealthiest imperial capital in the world, yet just a mile east of the financial district lay Whitechapel—a densely overcrowded district of narrow gas-lit alleys, workhouses, and shared lodging houses ("doss-houses") where thousands lived in extreme poverty.

Economic depression, severe unemployment, and anti-immigrant tensions created a volatile environment where street crime was common and police resources were stretched thin.

The Escalation & The Name Aug – Sept 1888

Scotland Yard opened the "Whitechapel Murders" file in April 1888 following attacks on Emma Elizabeth Smith and Martha Tabram. However, historians mark the true start of the Ripper series on August 31, 1888, with the murder of Mary Ann Nichols.

In late September 1888, the Central News Agency received a letter written in red ink signed "Jack the Ripper." Though modern historians believe a journalist likely fabricated the letter to boost newspaper sales, the nickname instantly stuck worldwide.

Chapter 02 // Origins & Genealogy

Who Were His Parents? The Unsolved Identity

The Direct Historical Answer Unknown

We do not know who Jack the Ripper's parents were because his true identity was never discovered. Unlike mythical villains with known backstories, "Jack the Ripper" was a media alias given to an unidentified perpetrator.

In 1888, policing lacked modern forensic science—there was no DNA profiling, no blood-type matching, and fingerprinting was not yet adopted by Scotland Yard. Without a verified name or birth record, his parentage and childhood remain completely unknown.

What Investigators & Suspects Suggest Historical Profile

Contemporary police surgeons and modern criminal profilers concluded the killer was likely a local male resident of Whitechapel who blended easily into the neighborhood, held a steady daytime job, and knew its labyrinth of alleys intimately.

Over 100 suspects have been proposed—ranging from troubled local laborers like Aaron Kosminski (son of Polish-Jewish immigrants Abram and Golda) to educated professionals like Montague John Druitt (son of Dorset surgeon William Druitt). None has ever been proven beyond doubt.

Mary Ann "Polly" Nichols

Location: Buck's Row (now Durward Street), Whitechapel

Born in London in 1845, Mary Ann Nichols was a mother of five whose life was destabilized by poverty and alcohol following the breakdown of her marriage. Unable to afford a four-penny bed at her Thrawl Street lodging house that night, she was killed in the early hours of August 31.

Historical Significance: Her death marked the unmistakable beginning of the Ripper panic and led Scotland Yard to assign senior detectives to Whitechapel.
Chapter 04 // Resolution & Theories

How It Ended—Or Did It?

NOVEMBER 9, 1888
The Sudden Stop
Following the murder of Mary Jane Kelly at Miller's Court—the only canonical attack committed indoors—the Ripper's killings abruptly ceased. No confirmed canonical murders occurred after November 1888.
1889 – 1891
False Alarms & Later Cases
A few subsequent Whitechapel murders (such as Alice McKenzie in 1889 and Frances Coles in 1891) sparked brief fears that the killer had returned, but investigators concluded they were unrelated crimes.
1892 & BEYOND
Official Closure of the File
Scotland Yard officially closed the Whitechapel Murders investigation in 1892 without making an arrest or bringing charges. While the crimes ended in 1888, the historical mystery never did.

Why Historians Believe the Killings Stopped Leading Theories

Serial offenders rarely stop voluntarily without an external intervention. Most historians agree the murders ended in late 1888 for one of four practical reasons:

1. Death: In an era of high mortality from tuberculosis, pneumonia, and street accidents, the killer may have died shortly after November 1888.

2. Institutionalization: Police memos from the 1890s noted a prime suspect had been committed to a Victorian lunatic asylum, ending his ability to roam Whitechapel.

3. Imprisonment or Emigration: He may have been jailed for an unrelated theft or assault, or boarded a ship out of London's busy docks to America or Australia.
